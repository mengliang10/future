Biotech has a credibility problem with generalist investors. The sector has produced decades of "breakthrough" announcements that dissolved in Phase III trials, and the timelines between discovery and commercial product are long enough to exhaust most investors' patience. The current wave of AI-enabled drug discovery is different - not because the science is guaranteed to work, but because the bottleneck has shifted from biology to computing, and that is a bottleneck we know how to solve.

## The Part That Is Already Real

Start with what is already commercially deployed. In-vivo CRISPR gene therapy - delivering CRISPR editing machinery to living patients to correct disease-causing mutations - received FDA approval for TTR amyloidosis in 2023. This is not a clinical trial. It is a commercial product that physicians can prescribe and patients can receive.

This approval matters beyond the specific disease because it answers the question regulators and the market had been holding open for a decade: can CRISPR therapies be safe and effective enough for approval? The answer is yes. The approval pathway is now established. Every subsequent in-vivo CRISPR program runs a shorter regulatory gauntlet because the category has cleared the foundational hurdle.

**[CRISPR Therapeutics](/stocks/CRSP/)** is the equity most directly exposed to this transition. Their CTX001/Casgevy program - in partnership with Vertex - was the first approved CRISPR therapy, for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia. The commercial launch is ongoing. Beyond the current product, CRISPR Therapeutics has a pipeline of in-vivo programs targeting diseases where conventional drug development has produced no effective options. The market is still pricing this primarily as a development-stage biotech despite having a commercial product generating revenue.

## The Part That Is Coming

AI drug discovery is the next wave, and it is roughly 18 months behind gene therapy in commercial maturity. The thesis is straightforward: drug discovery involves searching a near-infinite chemical and biological space for molecules that are safe, selective, and manufacturable. AI models can search that space orders of magnitude faster than traditional high-throughput screening. AlphaFold's protein structure predictions - now covering essentially the entire known proteome - are the foundational dataset that makes this tractable.

**[Recursion Pharmaceuticals](/stocks/RXRX/)** is running the most ambitious implementation: a fully integrated drug discovery platform that uses computer vision on cellular imaging, large biology foundation models, and robot-executed wet lab validation. The company has active partnerships with Bayer, Roche, and others who are essentially buying access to the platform rather than a specific drug. The risk is platform translation - does the AI-predicted candidate actually perform in patients? Phase II data over the next two years will answer this.

**[Moderna](/stocks/MRNA/)** is a different expression of the same thesis. The mRNA platform - proven at global scale during COVID - is now being applied to personalised cancer vaccines, RSV, influenza, and rare diseases. The manufacturing infrastructure is built. The regulatory precedent is established. The question is whether mRNA works as broadly across disease areas as the COVID data implied. The pipeline has enough shots on goal that partial success across indications is the most likely scenario.

**[Illumina](/stocks/ILMN/)** is the infrastructure layer. Whole genome sequencing costs have fallen dramatically, enabling population-scale genomics that feeds AI drug discovery models. Every AI biology company needs sequencing data. Illumina has built a near-monopoly in next-generation sequencing that is structurally hard to displace despite its attempts to maintain it through M&A (the Grail saga) rather than technology. The stock has been depressed by the regulatory overhang from the forced Grail divestiture - which creates a potentially interesting entry point if the core sequencing business is what you are buying.

## The Honest Risk

Gene therapy and AI drug discovery share a common failure mode: the gap between preclinical success and clinical success. Animal models and cell assays have predicted human outcomes correctly often enough to justify development, but failed often enough to make the asset-by-asset risk substantial. The correct posture is to own the platform companies - those with multiple shots on goal across disease areas - rather than single-asset bets.

The structural tailwind is durable. Healthcare spending is the largest sector of the global economy. The tools for biological intervention are becoming dramatically more powerful and precise. The companies building those tools at scale are identifiable. The timeline to full commercial maturity is longer than most quarterly-driven investors can tolerate - which is exactly why the opportunity exists for those with a longer horizon.
