Most macro commentary is pattern-matching dressed up as causal reasoning. Someone notices that yield curves inverted before three of the last five recessions and calls it a leading indicator. What that misses is the mechanism — *why* does the yield curve predict recessions, and through exactly what chain of cause and effect?

We built a research pipeline that ingests academic papers, Federal Reserve speeches, BIS research documents, and central bank publications, then extracts and validates the causal mechanisms explicitly stated in the text. The result is a database of causal rules ranked by how consistently they appear across independent research — not by how often we observe the correlation, but by how well-understood the mechanism is.

Here is what the most robust rules in the database say about the current environment.

## The Most Reliable Signal in Macroeconomics

The single most robust rule in our database — appearing consistently across hundreds of independent research papers spanning different eras, different countries, and different methodological approaches — is the relationship between the yield curve (specifically the 10-year minus 2-year Treasury spread) and future GDP growth.

The mechanism, as explained across that body of research: when long-term rates fall below short-term rates, banks' net interest margins compress or invert, tightening credit conditions at the retail and small business level. This monetary transmission mechanism works with a lag of roughly six months to two years. The yield curve is not magic — it is a summary statistic for credit availability in an economy, and credit availability precedes economic growth with a well-understood delay.

The current environment: the 10Y-2Y spread has recently moved back into positive territory after the longest inversion since the 1980s. If the historical mechanism holds, this normalisation precedes a growth re-rating over the next 18 to 36 months. The equity sectors that benefit most from re-rating during normalisation historically include financials (lending margins expand), industrials (capital expenditure increases), and consumer discretionary (spending confidence improves).

## The Dollar-Gold Relationship

The second most robust cluster of rules in our database involves the US dollar index (DXY) and gold. The mechanism: gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar increases the local-currency cost of gold for non-US buyers, suppressing demand. The deeper driver, however, is real yields — the return available on Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS). When real yields are high, the opportunity cost of holding gold (which pays no yield) is high. When real yields are negative or very low, gold becomes the better store of purchasing power.

The practical implication: watching DXY to predict gold is second-order. Watching real yields (TIPS) is first-order. In an environment where the Fed is cutting rates faster than inflation is normalising — producing declining real yields — gold has historically performed regardless of what the dollar is doing.

## Credit Spreads and Value Rotation

The third significant rule cluster involves high-yield credit spreads and equity factor performance. When high-yield spreads compress — meaning credit markets are pricing less default risk — the research consistently shows that value-style equities outperform growth-style equities over a one-to-three-month horizon.

The mechanism: credit spread compression signals improved confidence in corporate cash flows and balance sheets. This benefits value companies disproportionately because their valuations are more sensitive to near-term earnings certainty than growth companies, whose valuations depend primarily on long-duration future cash flows.

Current environment: credit spreads are near multi-year lows. If the compression holds or deepens, the factor models suggest value rotation continues — which is a positioning consideration if your portfolio is heavily skewed toward high-multiple technology.

## What This Framework Cannot Do

A causal rule database cannot tell you what markets will do tomorrow. It can tell you which mechanisms have been robust enough across different historical periods to be worth monitoring, and it can identify when those mechanisms are aligned or in conflict with current market conditions.

The rules described above have strong research support precisely because they describe structural features of how monetary policy transmits to the real economy — features that persist across business cycles. They are useful for thinking about positioning over quarters and years. They are not useful for timing entry points in weeks or days.

The most important use of this kind of analysis is not finding new edges. It is avoiding confident predictions about macro outcomes that have weak causal underpinning — the kind of commentary that sounds authoritative but is actually just recent correlation extrapolated forward.
