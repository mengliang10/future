
# Europe

Europe's technology position is unusual: it holds chokepoints in equipment and regulation, and very few platform companies. That combination produces a specific kind of influence — rules and tools rather than products — and a specific kind of fragility, because neither is a large employer of engineers at scale.

## The equipment chokepoint

ASML is the single most important company in advanced manufacturing. High-NA EUV, at well over €300m per scanner, decides when sub-2nm patterning becomes economical, and the export-licensing regime around it is a foreign-policy instrument. imec, the Belgian research institute, is the neutral testbed where the industry de-risks the next three generations; its pilot line is often the first place a new tool is integrated.

## Rules as industrial policy

The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024, with general-purpose model obligations from August 2025 and high-risk duties phased through 2027. The Chips Act committed €43bn and a target of 20% of global production by 2030, up from roughly 10%. Neither is a guarantee of capability, but both shape procurement: vendors document to EU rules because their customers sell into Europe, which makes the Act an export standard in practice.

## Energy and quantum

Europe's fusion position spans the public and private: ITER in France is a burning-plasma research programme with a mid-2030s first-plasma target, while Tokamak Energy in the UK is testing whether high-temperature superconducting magnets can make a compact spherical tokamak viable. Rolls-Royce's 470 MWe SMR was selected for the UK programme in 2025, with first power in the 2030s.

In quantum, IQM sells on-premises superconducting systems to HPC centres, Pasqal scales neutral-atom arrays for simulation, and Oxford Quantum Circuits sells cloud access to a UK-built machine. These are credible research companies competing for the same public budgets and the same scarce cryogenic engineers.

## Autonomy

Wayve represents the software-first bet: an end-to-end driving stack licensed to automakers rather than operated as a fleet. It raised US$1.05bn in 2024, and its hardest problem is validation, not capability — a learned system is harder to inspect when it fails.

## What would change this view

A credible European platform company in AI, evidence that Chips Act money produced qualified output rather than construction, or a high-risk AI enforcement action that resets compliance costs. Absent those, Europe's influence will keep running through tools and rules rather than products.
